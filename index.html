Limitless Accessible Aircraft · ICAT Interdisciplinary Projects · mentored by a Boeing seats integration engineer · Aug 2025 - Aug 2026

Limitless

Powered wheelchairs fly in the cargo hold. If the joystick breaks, the chair can't be driven, and the passenger lands without their mobility. We designed a protector that rides on the chair through every handoff.

1 / 6
Role
Industrial designer: concept sketches, 3D renders, visual communication
Team
Fall 2025 team of 3: industrial design, industrial & systems engineering, electrical engineering
Mentors
Boeing seats integration engineer · All Wheels Up board member (retired Boeing seating) · parent of a wheelchair user
Timeframe
Aug 2025 - Aug 2026
Tools
Blender, sketching, whiteboard mind mapping

Problem

Damage rarely comes from one big event. It comes from many small ones. Handlers drop chairs onto conveyor belts, bump them into cargo walls, and tip them on their side to fit through short cargo doors. The joystick sits at the front edge of the armrest, takes most of those hits, and can't be detached like a headrest can.

$500-5kto replace one joystick
3-6 ftdrops onto concrete or conveyors to survive
−65 to 160°Fcargo-hold temperature range
< 60 starget install time, no tools

My role

I sketched and modeled the concepts, turned them into Blender renders for mentor reviews, and kept the team's meeting notes. I also helped present every week to our mentors, including a Boeing seats integration engineer.

Process

  1. Narrow to the part that strands people

    We first split up across components, and I took the headrest. A mentor whose son uses a wheelchair pointed out that headrests can be removed and carried on board, but joysticks can't. So we moved the whole team onto the joystick.

  2. Write requirements from the cargo hold

    Survive 3-6 ft drops and sideways crushing. Hold up from −65°F to 160°F and from sea level to 40,000 ft, and resist de-icing fluid and fuel. For the passenger, it has to install in under a minute, one-handed, with no tools, and be small enough to pack.

  3. Map the whole problem

    Mapped design approaches, materials, fasteners, regulations (FAA, DOT, IATA) and environmental hazards in one place, so we could connect damage patterns to solutions.

  4. Generate and model concepts

    Ran two-hour concept sessions twice a week. Three directions came out of them: a rigid case, a flexible shell with a TPU grommet, and a two-part sleeve. I modeled them in Blender for review.

  5. Widen the scope

    Mentor feedback and damage cases showed that the screen, wiring and mounts fail along with the joystick. We expanded the brief to cover the whole control area.

Key insight

The protector doesn't need to survive a drop from space. It needs to tell a rushed handler "fragile medical equipment."

High-visibility color and clear labeling became requirements alongside impact protection, because a visible cue changes how people handle the chair.

Outcome

We delivered three developed concepts, a set of design requirements, and test metrics for the next team. The metrics include drop and compression tests, fit across 3-5 wheelchair models, and an install time under 60 seconds. The two-year plan moves to down-selection and detailed design in spring 2026, then building and testing in fall 2026.

Where it took me next: in summer 2026 I started asking how economy travel, on trains and in airports as well as planes, could feel considered instead of stripped down. That is an open question I'm carrying forward, not a finished project.

VTID Transportation Task Force × Collins Aerospace · Aug - Dec 2024

Energy-Efficient Cooling for In-Flight Comfort

Economy seats run warm, and cooling them costs energy an airline is trying to cut. We looked for a cooler seat that still fits Collins Aerospace's goal of a carbon-neutral cabin.

Role
Cabin Systems Architect
Partner
Collins Aerospace
Program
VTID Transportation Task Force, 12 students
Reviewers
Collins Aerospace executives
Timeframe
Aug - Dec 2024

Team problem statement

"Enhance the user's in-flight experience in a way that contributes to Collins Aerospace's mission of a carbon-neutral cabin through materiality, form, and cooling technology."

Passengers feel heat most where their body meets the seat. Cooling that surface adds weight, power and parts, and all three work against the carbon-neutral goal. The design had to improve comfort without spending more energy than it saves.

My role

I worked on a student team that developed three seating concepts and presented them to Collins Aerospace executives. My focus was the ergonomics: mapping how 5th to 95th percentile passengers use an economy seat, and finding space that could be given back to them.

Three constraints we designed against

Comfort

  • Human thermodynamics
  • Headrest, seat form and size
  • Where cooling sits, and in what material
  • Controls the passenger can find and use

Safety

  • FAA rules (14 CFR, Chapter I)
  • Weight and approved materials
  • Spills, crash loads and body fluids
  • Fit with existing cabin structure and cost

Sustainability

  • Lightweight, durable, easy to clean
  • Cooling efficiency and emissions
  • Replaceable parts, simple assembly
  • Less material waste

Process

  1. Benchmark heated and cooled seats

    Compared four systems across aviation and cars: Caynova and Gentherm for Lufthansa, Lantal for SWISS, Katzkin, and the Lexus NX 350.

  2. Take a real seat apart

    Took apart an economy row down to the frame to see where cooling hardware, wiring and controls could actually fit.

  3. Map the passengers

    Mapped 5th to 95th percentile passengers against the seat to find unused space, and set a rule that any gain had to reach every body size.

  4. Develop three directions

    Developed three concepts with fluid cooling at the seat surface and reviewed them with Collins Aerospace executives.

What the teardown showed

We measured and documented every part of the row before designing anything. The numbers below set the space a cooling system has to live in.

Seat width
19 in (15 in between armrests)
Seat depth
21 in
Cushion
5 × 21 × 19 in, doubles as a flotation device
Back height
29 in · 47 in overall
Under-seat box
3 × 8 × 8 in life vest box on the seat base
Row width
58 in for three seats

Structure is carbon-fiber laminate over a foam core, for both the seat base and the back. Recline runs from an armrest button, through a cable, to a gas strut, like a bike brake, and the whole row shares one electrical circuit. Any cooling hardware has to fit around the life vest box and that wiring without adding a new failure point.

Outcome

The team presented three concepts to Collins Aerospace executives. They project a 4°C lower seat-surface temperature and 8% more living space without removing a seat.

Inclusive Interiors · Undergraduate Research × ICAT · Sep 2026 - Present

Swap the core,
not the cane.

CoreGrip is a white-cane grip with a swappable core: one for summer heat and sweat, one for winter cold. It's built so a blind person never has to trade comfort for what the cane tells them.

In progress · exhibiting in the ICAT Cube, spring 2027

A white cane is a sensor.
The hand holding it can't go in a pocket.

01 · Explore

Seven questions before one answer.

I pitched my way in. I emailed the professor proposing sensory-based immersive audio for the exhibition, backed by three samples of my sound and research work. Once in, I didn't lock onto an idea. I ran seven scenarios and mapped every connection that surfaced, including the ones I wasn't sure were realistic.

  1. The Dark RoomA blind visitor enters the Cube alone.
  2. The HeadsetA wheelchair user meets a standing-only VR station.
  3. Too MuchAn autistic teen needs to come down mid-exhibit.
  4. Eye HeightA 3'6" child and a grandparent with a walker at one panel.
  5. The CockpitControls you use in the dark, under stress.
  6. Gate to SeatA powered-wheelchair user's worst boarding moment.
  7. Thermal ComfortA dry surface you can rest your palms on to cool down.

02 · Edit

Knowing what to leave out.

My first answer to almost every scenario was sound. I build 8D audio that moves around the listener, so it was the obvious move. I chose to make it supporting rather than the whole idea. I cut mist too. Unexpected water on your skin, when you can't see where it's coming from, overstimulates and leaves someone to clean up. And I dropped the boarding scenario, because I'd already designed wheelchair joystick cases on Limitless and wanted new ground.

Sound as the whole ideaKept as a supporting layer
Mist coolingMust be dry: no leaks, no cleanup
Another protective caseAlready explored on Limitless
Discomfort that existing fixes missThe direction I chose

03 · Insight

“Something designed to solve one problem can create another problem.”

Because of thyroid-related sweating, I've ruined notebooks mid-sentence, and textured grips stop working once my palm is wet. Gloves in winter make my hands sweat too. So I asked what happens to a blind person's grip at 100°F, and what happens in winter, when gloves warm the hand but muffle the vibrations the cane sends up from the floor.

04 · Research

The cane reads the ground. Weather changes the reading.

Researching snow, ice and rain showed me how much information comes through a cane: texture, sound and resistance. Weather affects more than comfort. It changes how well the cane works.

Already exists
Wool "mobility mittens" with a cane opening keep hands warm.
Already exists
A City University of Hong Kong grip uses heat as a navigation signal, not for comfort.
Already exists
Cork grips are standard on some canes.
The gap
I found nothing for summer heat and sweat, and no single grip that works across seasons.

05 · Design

One grip. Two cores.

It started with a portable cooler's interchangeable attachments and a motorcycle's heated grips. The question was whether a cane could change for the season while the cane itself stays the same.

Summer core

Draws heat out, dry.

A ventilated lattice with a recycled aluminum strip that touches the palm through a window in the cork. Passive: no power, no water.

Winter core

Warm without a glove.

Holds a certified rechargeable hand warmer behind a cork layer, on its lowest setting. The bare hand keeps its connection to the floor.

The swap

One hand. No sight needed.

A magnetic slide-and-click latch. A flat face along the grip supports the index-finger hold many cane users rely on.

06 · Exhibit

Feel the difference yourself.

A 24 × 48 in honeycomb-cardboard wall panel, tab-and-slot with no glue, holds two to three canes 36-44 in off the floor. In front of it, a flush strip of tile, carpet, rubber and cork lets visitors sweep a cane and feel each surface for themselves.

Built to 2010 ADA: 305.3 clear floor space · 308 reach range · 307.2 protrusion · 302/303 floor surface and ¼" level change · 309.4 one-hand operation · 703 raised characters and braille

07 · Test

The bar: more comfort, no less feedback.

Standard gripBaseline
Gloved handToday's winter fix
CoreGripThe proposal

Blindfolded participants will sweep the texture strip three ways. I'll count correct texture identifications, record hand comfort and sweat, weigh the prototype (every gram at the handle tires the wrist), and measure grip temperature with the warmer on.

Calibrate

Students get told they're a "visual learner" and stop there. Calibrate is an AI study coach that treats the study method as a hypothesis, then tests it on the student's own material.

Users tested

Timeframe
Jul 2026 - present
Tools

Problem

"Learning styles" are a popular idea with weak evidence behind them. Most AI study apps make it worse: they label you, generate flashcards, and never check whether anything worked. Our question was different. Which technique actually raises this student's scores in this subject?

My role

I mapped the full product loop, designed the screens, and set the UX direction for the team. I wrote the brief for how the product should look and feel, and ran the prototyping sprints with users.

Key design decisions

  1. Call it a hypothesis, not a label

    Onboarding asks how you study, one question at a time, in under a minute. The result screen says plainly that it's a starting guess to test.

  2. Make AI earn trust

    A teammate raised the risk of AI hallucinating questions. I answered it in the interface: every question is tagged Generated, Needs Edit or Approved, and only approved questions enter a study session.

  3. Ask why when a score drops

    A low score alone doesn't say what went wrong. A one-tap follow-up offers four reasons, and each one leads to a different next step.

  4. Don't overclaim results

    The comparison screen shows how many sessions it's based on and says when there isn't enough data yet.

Outcome

Calibrate is a working MVP. Students can paste notes, review AI questions, run a technique session, and see which technique is scoring best in each subject. Across three prototyping sprints, task completion rose from 60% to 90%.

SolarCar at Virginia Tech · Mechanical R&D, Human Factors · Sep 2026 - Present

Out of the car in under 10 seconds

A solar car driver sits low in a tight shell, wears a race helmet in the heat, and has to climb out unassisted in under ten seconds. Our two-person human factors group designs for that body, for today's car and the next one.

In progress · Present
Upcoming Egress testing on the Voyager chassis November 1, 2026
Role
Human Factors & Ergonomics, R&D (mechanical team)
Group
2-person human factors group within R&D
Vehicles
Voyager (current car, 1.8 m wide) · Gen 3 (next car)
Rulebook
American Solar Challenge 2026 Regulations

The rules set the brief

Racing regulations turn ergonomics into pass/fail. These four set our work:

< 10 sunassisted exit, standing clear of the car, wheels not chocked (Reg 9.8.A)
835 mmtorso clearance arc from the hip point (Reg 10.3.C)
50thpercentile male that clearance is based on, without a helmet
1continuous perimeter required for the steering wheel (Reg 10.7.A)

The clearance rule is sized for an average man without a helmet. Anyone taller, or anyone wearing the helmet the rules require, needs more room than the minimum. That's the same percentile thinking I used in economy seating, applied here with a stopwatch.

What I'm working on

  1. Faster egress from Voyager · testing November 1, 2026

    The chassis is set at ride height with a mock-up of the future padding and a placeholder topshell. Timed exits on November 1 will show where the body gets caught.

  2. Steering wheel prototypes · prototyping

    Rough prototypes for fit and grip, plus a plan for how the wheel is held during egress testing. The rules warn that hobby-printed wheels rarely pass inspection, so the printed ones are for testing feel, not for racing.

  3. Suit, helmet and fabrics · research

    Comparing bodysuit materials, helmets that meet the required Snell/FIA ratings, and fabrics that reduce driver heat strain.

  4. Gen 3 cockpit geometry · long term

    Using what we learn from Voyager to set the cockpit dimensions for the next car.

Why it matters for cabins

It's the cabin problem at its tightest: fit a body into the smallest legal space, keep it cool, and get it out fast.

Short analyses of other designers' work, written from the inside of a neurodivergent body. This is where I work out ideas before they turn into projects.

Analysis · 2026 · 8 min read Subject: Neurodiversity Architecture Network (NAN)

What I feel when I see your work: a neurodivergent designer's read of three NAN pods

I was diagnosed with ADHD at 10 and have always felt space physically. I wrote this analysis of three neuro-inclusive pods by the Neurodiversity Architecture Network and sent it to NAN's founder, who responded warmly.

Pod images: Neurodiversity Architecture Network, upscaled for display. Shown for commentary.

Direction over enclosure

"It is not about the amount of enclosed space but about the directness of the angles targeting specific physical regions of the body." The open-sided pod feels secure because the head pressure does the work that closed sides never could.

The neck pillow principle

On a plane, a neck pillow isn't only comfort. It locks your focal point. "The pillow does not trap you, it gives your nervous system permission to stop searching." A curved wall does the same thing for a whole body.

Predictable beats random

A massage chair that follows a set pattern regulates you. A massage gun moved at random doesn't. Directional pressure that repeats feels secure instead of overwhelming.

Imperfection reads as alive

The yarn coils aren't perfectly uniform, so the pods feel "already in motion" even when empty. Flawless construction would read as decor.

What I'd change

The third pod's diagonal opening at the top releases the eye into too much space at once. I'd make it a straight vertical opening. If the outward flare stays, I'd add a fishnet layer high above: flat, it filters the light; domed, it arches with the body. Either way the eye gets a place to land, an openness that still has edges.

Same instinct, two years earlier

Before I'd seen NAN's work, I sketched a desk enclosure for my own room. It had a shelf-style organizer with a head-space canopy whose transparent ceiling levels adjust in height. That's the same breathable ceiling and the same avoidance of beige I found in the pods. The sketch later became a formal academic project.

It also ties to my cabin work. I wrote that a cramped airplane seat, with feet on a backpack and a neck pillow behind, actually grounds me: contained from below and from above. Perceived space isn't about square footage.
